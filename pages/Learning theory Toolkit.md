- ## Theories
	- ### McDermott [[McDermott Acquisition of a Child]]
	  id:: 6a8db632-a7be-491e-8b95-318a2495170a
		- What does this allow me to see?
			- Disability and dysfunction as a result of a system upon a student as opposed to a student's fit within a system.
				- In particular, a system not designed with the child's behavior in mind.
		- What might it obscure?
			- Explicitly obscures folk theory that a student is not good for a system, and takes away blame from the child. This is good to an extent.
			- This forgiveness does go against grit and determination; albeit in a case where going against these ideas is helpful.
		- When is this especially useful?
			- Children with learning disabilities (ADHD, Autism, hearing problems, etc.) and how to understand them where they are *in a system*
		- How does it compare to other ways of understanding learning?

		- First take:
			- This is something I knew innately but never really thought of the wording for - learning disabilities only apply because of a context in which a person is labeled dysfunctional for the system. In this case, it was Adam in the american public school system.
		- Important:
			- McDermott notes that in other cultures (or in systems where the student is given a situation that applaudes to their learning over time as opposed to their learning rate), Adam would not be considered LD.
			- "The world was precisely organized for making his disability apparent, that he was the negative achievement of a school system that insisted that everyone do better than everyone else."
			- Adam's visibility as someone with LD increases with the demands of the education system.

		- Reading Questions:
		- *McDermott describes Adam across four settings.*
		  *Pick the single transition between two settings that you think is most revealing — the moment where the contrast between "Adam in Setting A" and "Adam in Setting B" does the most work for McDermott's argument. Which transition did you pick, and why does that particular contrast matter more (to you) than the others?*
			- On this:
				- The author shows that different settings produced different Adams
					- Everyday life as "undemanding"
						- Everyday life being unstructured and allowing Adam to complete tasks in his own way - example used was remembering a phone number
					- Cooking class similarly so if Peter was there - without Peter, he found himself troubled
					- Classes had him acting out and struggling to maintain his attention or interest
						- Classes have a share of tools to use - notes, friends - but is demanding of attention
						- Additionally, is it a place of great ridicule
						- This was easier for him with Peter around - lifting an extraneous load perhaps?
					- Tests had him gobsmacked and acting out - Throwing down his pencil, "It's too hard!"
						- The resources he usually used were not at his disposal
			- The transition from classes to tests, where he has to focus more on getting an answer than understanding a question, feels the most revealing to me and resonates with me more as well.
			  It shows that his trouble in class and his trouble on the tests is not the same function, his approach to both
